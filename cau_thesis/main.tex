\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

The ever increasing power generation attributed to renewable energy sources (RES),
predominantly in Germany within Europe, with a current share of 57,2 \% of produced
gross-electricity by RES (2025)\footnote{See Data from \cite{ageb_bruttostromerzeugung_2026}, last accessed: 2026-05-13 19:08.},
highlights the rapid decarbonization of the energy sector.\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.2]{eurelectric2026shortterm}}
By regulatory design, RES have a feed-in priority\footnote{See \cite{eu_red_2018} for the relevant EU directive and \cite{ec_recommendation_supportschemes_2024} for the support scheme recommendation, as well as the national EEG \cite{eeg_2023_feed_in}.}, meaning that they are guaranteed to be
integrated into the electricity grid and are given priority over other sources of electricity
generation, thereby directly impacting the price formation process.

As the RES production, as just one of many influencial external
variables, is itself heavily weather dependent, this uncertainty is
transferred into the (european) electricity markets, resulting in
increased price volatility and a growing demand for balancing the
short term imbalances as well as forecasting errors in the continuous
intraday markets (CID), where the financial trading of these
positions is carried out.

\section{The financialization of the electricity market}\label{sec:financialization}
% \section{Electronic markets for electricity}\label{sec:electronic_markets}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
In Europe, the main exchanges for trading electricity are the
Intercontinental Exchange (ICE), the NordPool, the European Energy
Exchange (EEX) and the European Power Exchange (EPEX SPOT). The
latter one, acting as the reference spot market, where the short term
trading of electricity takes place, what this thesis focuses on.

These exchanges provide a platform for trading electricity contracts,
including futures, options, and spot contracts. The spot trading is
organized in a CID market and three intraday auctions (IDA), where
the CID allows for trading of electricity contracts with delivery
within the next 24 hours, while the IDA is a single auction that
takes place at a specific times each day, allowing for trading of
electricity contracts with delivery on the following day. The CID
market offers 1-hour contracts, 30-Minute contracts, and the most
granular 15-Minute contracts, covering the entire 24-hour period of
the next following day. The IDA, on the other hand, focuses on 1-hour
contracts for the next day, providing a platform for market
participants to adjust their positions based on updated information
and forecasts. More details about the market structure and trading
mechanics of the EPEX SPOT will be provided in chapter
\hyperref[sec:structure_european_power_market]{2.1 The structure of
    the European Power Market}.

The trading in these contracts is facilitated through a limit order
book (LOB), where buyers and sellers submit their orders, which are
then matched based on price and time priority. The LOB provides a
transparent view of the market, allowing participants to see the
available liquidity and make informed trading decisions. This
\textit{order-driven market} structure is a key feature of the
electricity markets, as it allows for efficient price discovery and
facilitates the trading of electricity contracts in a competitive
environment, in contrast to a \textit{quote-driven market} structure,
where market makers provide liquidity by quoting buy and sell prices
for the contracts. The dynamics of the LOB are influenced by various
factors, including the arrival of limit orders and market orders,
cancellations, and modifications of existing orders. In order for a
smooth operating and liquid market, it is crucial to have a
sufficient number of market participants, including so called market
makers, who provide liquidity by continuously quoting buy and sell
prices for the contracts, making sure incoming orders can be executed
efficiently at all times. Liquidity provision is carried out by these
market makers at a rapid speed pocketing the bid-ask spread, which is
the difference between the highest price a buyer is willing to pay
and the lowest price a seller is willing to accept. As the bid-ask
spread is usually only a few cents wide, at best, earning this gap
has to be done several thousands of times a day, which is only
possible through the use of algorithmic trading strategies, which are
often based on statistical and machine learning techniques, designed
to quickly analyze huge amounts of market data, identify trading
opportunities, and execute trades at high speeds. With an average
daily order volume of 1,242,762 orders in the EPEX SPOT CID market in
2019, this leaves no real other options, as to make use of
algorithmic trading strategies, to be able to compete in this
market.\footnote{2019 figures, see \cite{epexspot_annual_report_2019}
    for more details.} This process is essential for maintaining an
efficient and liquid market, as it allows for quick execution of
trades and helps to reduce price volatility. The presence of market
makers also encourages more participants to enter the market, as they
can be assured of liquidity and competitive pricing. In recent years,
the role of market makers has become increasingly important in the
electricity markets, with many participants often using algorithmic
trading strategies, what is also evident in the share of submitted
orders to the EPEX SPOT via Application Programming Interfaces
(APIs), that is around 65\% and represents around 47\% of traded
volume.\footnote{2019 figures, see \cite{epexspot_annual_report_2019}
    for more details, as of 20-05-2026 455 Exchange Members are
    registered and 4XX APIs.}

The discrepancy between automatically and human interacted manual
trading volume is due that the automatically submitted orders are
often smaller in size, but more frequent, while the manually
submitted orders are often larger in size, but less frequent, what
later motivates modelling decisisons with respect to variable order
sizes.

This financialization highlights the economic importance of efficient
trading strategies and risk management techniques in the electricity
markets, as market participants seek to optimize their returns while
managing the risks associated with price volatility and uncertainty.
The unique characteristics of the electricity markets, such as the
non-storability of electricity and the influence of weather
conditions on supply and demand, further emphasize the need for
sophisticated modeling approaches that can capture these dynamics
effectively. In this context, the application of Hawkes processes for
optimal market making in intraday electricity markets offers a
promising avenue for developing advanced trading strategies that can
adapt to the complex and dynamic nature of these markets, what this
thesis essentially covers.

Here the main overall approach resembles around the idea that the LOB
can be treated as stochastic system, also referred to as
\textit{zero-intelligence model} but will be enhanced by including
some priors like non-constant order intensities or dependency
structures \footnote{See \cite{jain2024limit}.}, in contrast to the
often used second main approach, treating the LOB as a strategic game
between market participants, where the actions of one participant can
influence the actions of others, leading to a complex interplay of
strategies and counter-strategies, also known as \textit{perfect
    rational agent-based model}. By modeling the LOB as a stochastic
queueing system, we can also allow for flexible modelling techniques
for the involved order types, that can be submitted \textbf{market
    orders} (MO), \textbf{Limit Orders} (LO) and \textbf{cancellations},
which are the main drivers of the LOB dynamics and are explained in
more detail in \hyperref[sub_sec:order_types]{2.2.1 Order
    Types}.\footnote{See \cite{gould2013limit}.}

\section{Main Research Objectives of the Thesis}\label{sec:main_research_objectives}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The main objective of this thesis is to explore the application of
Hawkes processes for optimal market making in intraday electricity
markets. Specifically, we aim to develop a modeling framework that
captures the dynamics of the limit order book (LOB) and the
interactions between different order types, such as limit orders,
market orders, and cancellations. By leveraging the self-exciting
nature of Hawkes processes, we seek to model the arrival of orders
and their impact on the LOB, allowing us to develop trading
strategies that can adapt to the evolving market conditions. Through
empirical analysis and numerical simulations, we aim to evaluate the
performance of our proposed approach and compare it with traditional
market making strategies, ultimately contributing to the advancement
of trading techniques in the context of intraday electricity markets.
Taking into account the often not properly considered dynamics of
Cross excitation, time varying baseline intensities and general
intensities, external excitement through updated forecasts and
external variables, we seek to develop a comprehensive modeling
framework that can effectively capture the complex dynamics of the
LOB and provide insights into optimal market making strategies in the
context of intraday electricity markets. The main research questions
that this thesis seeks to address are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item How can Hawkes processes be effectively applied to model the dynamics
          of the limit order book in intraday electricity markets?
    \item What are the key stylized facts of the limit order book in the EPEX
          SPOT CID market, and how can they be incorporated into the modeling
          framework?
    \item How does the performance of the proposed Hawkes process-based market
          making strategy compare to traditional market making strategies in
          terms of profitability and risk management?
    \item What are the implications of the findings for practitioners in the
          energy trading sector, and how can they be used to inform trading
          strategies and risk management techniques in intraday electricity
          markets?
\end{itemize}

In answering the above objectives, the main contributions are

This thesis is aimed at obtaining the optimal, dynamic quote
distances from the best bid/ask using stochastic optimal control
theory solving the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation. This is
done, proposing different, extending modelling frameworks, all making
use of the multivariate marked Hawkes model with time-varying
baseline intensities. The bid and ask side will be modelled
separately, allowing for interactions, as well as per side, then the
three main components, affecting price building, namely Market Order
Arrivals, Limit Order Arrivals and Cancellations.By starting from
this baseline model framework with six (interaction) channels, it
will be extended to the main model by also incorporating the first
three price levels per side. Two additional extensions will be
reviewed, namely the \textit{state-dependent} and the \textit{marked}
one. In the state-dependent extension, the intensities will be
dependent on parameters like imbalance, spread, queue size,
inventory, time to delivery and time of day. In the marked extension,
order size and price impact will be used as marks, especially for MO
and LO. A possible optional extension could be the use of Deep
Reinforcement Learning (DRL), since especially in the state-dependent
extensions, we face a rich state space, which can be hard to solve
through traditional methods, but is well suited for DRL, which can be
used to learn optimal policies. For the estimation strategy, the
maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) will be the main workhorse, but
we will consider also non-parametric spline based estimation for the
excitation kernel functions.

the International Commodities Exchange (ICE) and the Nasdaq OMX are
competing for trading volumes for a broader range of delivery areas.

\subsection{The Day-Ahead Market}\label{sub_sec:day_ahead_market}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
In contrast to the Derivatives Market of the EEX, where the traded
contracts are primarily settled financially, a physical delivery is
mandatory on the Day-Ahead Market (also referred to as \textit{Spot
    Market}).\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.
        136]{bnetza_monitoringreport_energy_2025}} The Day-Ahead Market is a
sealed-bid, uniform price, multi-unit implicit
auction.\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.233]{balardy2022empirical}} Its
main purpose is to already lock in certain volumes for the next day
(D). Based on newly arrived forecasts, updated information and the
need to balance out the supply and demand, market participants can
adjust their positions in the Intraday Market. Market participants
can submit so-called bidding curves, stating precisely how much power
they would want to generate or consume at each price tick between the
minimum and maximum prices at each operational subperiod of the next
following day, i.e. 24 hours, 30 Minutes and 15 Minutes, resulting in
96 subperiods in total. Submitted prices at which the produces would
want to sell power is by regulatory design set to the marginal cost
of production, that dictates a natural merit-order hierarchy of the
different production technologies. RES naturally operate at the
lowest marginal costs, often assumed to be zero, therefore occupying
the lowest price levels and the left side of the supply curve, while
conventional power plants, such as coal, gas and nuclear, operate at
higher marginal costs, occupying the higher price levels and the
right side of the supply curve. These biddingcurves have to be
submitted up until the gate closure time, which is typically around
12:00 CET on the day before delivery (D-1), allowing them to react to
the latest information. Most general Market areas are further divided
into bidding zones, which are defined by the transmission system
operators (TSOs) and represent areas with similar supply and demand
conditions. The geographical area of Germany is divided into four
control areas, according to the four TSOs, which are TenneT (DE1),
50Hertz (DE2), Amprion (DE3) and TransnetBW (DE4), that are encoded
in the send orders as can be seen in the data. The price formation is
then carried out through a uniform price auction, where the market
clearing price is determined by the intersection of the aggregated
supply and demand curves, which are formed by the submitted orders
(for every granularity of the next following day, i.e. 96 15-Min
subperiods). The market clearing price is the price at which the
total quantity of electricity demanded equals the total quantity
supplied, and all transactions are executed at this price. It also
serves as a reference price for the Intraday Market and the Balancing
Market, as it reflects the market's expectations for the next day.
All power producers that have submitted orders at or below the market
clearing price will be scheduled to produce electricity, and are
awarded the market clearing price for their production. An exemplary
supply and demand curve for the market area DE-LU can be seen in
\hyperref[fig:day_ahead_curves]{Figure 2.3}, for the delivery date of
the 28th of May, 00:00-00:15. To enhance european coupling,
optimizing cross border flow and ultimately welfare, the Day-Ahead
Market is coupled across the different bidding zones (Single Day
Ahead Coupling - SDAC), meaning that the market clearing price is
determined simultaneously across all bidding zones\footnote{Exact
    pricing is carried out by the EUPHEMIA algorithm.}, taking into
account the cross-border transmission capacities and the supply and
demand conditions in each zone, which is a crucial aspect for the
price formation process and the resulting price dynamics in the
Intraday Market, as the latter one is directly linked to the
Day-Ahead Market through the trading of the same contracts.

The exchanges landscape in Europe is here more fragmented, as there
are multiple smaller nominated electricity market operators (NEMOs)
that operate Day-Ahead Markets.

Microstructurally: batch market: aggregates liquidity in a single
point in time, it aggregates liquidity at a single point in time,
eliminating intra-auction adverse selection and any latency race (cf.
Budish et al., 2015). Its uniform price is the reference for the
entire downstream complex: futures settle against it, intraday prices
are quoted (in the empirical literature) as spreads to it, and many
support schemes and PPAs index to it. Order types include simple
hourly (now quarter-hourly) step and interpolated orders, block
orders (fill-or-kill across multiple MTUs, with linked and exclusive
variants), and, on some NEMOs, more exotic constructs (loop blocks,
big blocks, scalable complex orders); a storage order type is under
public consultation with expected SDAC go-live in Q4 2027 (ENTSO-E,
2026b).

\subsection{The Intraday Market}\label{sub_sec:intraday_market}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
As the physical delivery of the traded contracts is also mandatory on
the intraday market, purely financially motivated participants seek
to avoid not-netted positions heavily, as they would have to be
balanced out in the balancing market, which is the most expensive
one, as it is the last market before delivery. The main purpose of
the Intraday Market is to balance out the previously locked in
Day-Ahead positions\footnote{\cite[See ][,
        p.238]{balardy2022empirical}} that may deviate due to Power Plant
Outages, optimisation of power plant usage (generator), optimisation
of demand (costumer), renewable energy producer – changes of
forecasts\cite{kiesel_efc16_2016}. The CID Market starts on the day
before delivery (D-1) at 15:00 CET after closing of the Day-Ahead
Market with opening the order books for all 24 hours of the next
following day resulting in 24 books in parallel (with the hour 01:
00:00 - 01:00 being the first), 30 Minutes and 15 Minutes. The single
resolutions can be traded up until 5 Minutes before delivery. Due to
this nature the contract lifetime lasts from 8 hours and 55 Minutes
(for the first hour of the next following day D) up until 31 hours
and 55 Minutes (for the last hour of the next following day D).
Engaged participants therefore can trade up to 32 contracts
simultaneously, just considering the hourly
resolution.\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.234]{balardy2022empirical}} This
natural ordering of the hours over the course of the day, also groups
them inherently into ``peak' and ``off-peak' hours, stressing their
different dynamics during their contract lifetime for trading.

\section{Microstructure and Organisation of Intraday Power Trading}\label{sec:microstructure_and_organisation_of_power_trading}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
As the main trading takes place on-exchange and is not
broker-facilitated, the core price building mechanism is represented
by the LOB.\footnote{See \cite{bnetza_monitoringreport_energy_2025},
    where only 0.2 TWh of Intraday Trading was traded on broker platforms
    vs. 102 TWh on EPEX Spot.} Most academic research is centered around
data from highly liquid markets, such as equity or forex markets,
where the bid-ask spread is usually only the tick-size as the minimum
price-increment, that is possible. Therefore these models often only
concern the best bid and aks prices, as there is most volume and a
huge market-depth, often not entirely depleted by incoming market
orders. In stark contrast, the electricity markets are characterized
by a much wider bid-ask spread, that can be several times the
tick-size, and a much lower market depth, where incoming market
orders often deplete the entire volume at the best bid or ask price,
leading to a mid-price movement. This unique characteristic of the
electricity markets necessitates a different modeling approach that
can effectively capture the dynamics of the LOB and the resulting
price movements, which is what this thesis focuses on.

\subsection{Limit Order Books and Trading Mechanics}\label{sub_sec:limit_order_books_and_trading_mechanics}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The LOB is defined on a fixed discrete grid of prices (price levels),
where the step size of each price level is called \textit{tick or
    tick-size}. This tick-size in the CID market is 0.01 EUR/MWh, which
means that the price levels are discretized in increments of 0.01
EUR/MWh. The \textit{tick-size} will be denoted by $\Delta p$. The
respective minimum and maximum volumes for submitted orders are 0.1
MWh and 1000 MWh, which means that market participants can submit
orders for quantities within this range. The order-size will be
denoted by $V$.

The trading hours are 24/7, with specific gate closure times that
vary based on the delivery hour, as illustrated in
\hyperref[fig:european_power_market_design]{Figure 2.1}. All together
these parameters are called the resolution parameters of a LOB and
are summarised in \hyperref[tab:epex_cid_parameters]{Table 2.1}.

Starting from a basic market setup, participants can submit either
Limit Orders (LO) or Market Orders (MO). Participants, sending a MO
wish to immediately buy or sell their submitted volume at the best
price currently available. Due to this unconditional specification,
MOs are also referred to as \textit{aggressive orders}, as they are
willing to accept the current market price and reduce the resting
volumes at the best bid or ask price. Across the multiple, discrete
price levels, the best bid price is the highest price at which there
is a buy order, while the best ask price is the lowest price at which
there is a sell order.

\begin{definition}[Best bid and ask prices]
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Best Bid}(t) = \max\{p_i | q_i > 0, p_i < \text{Best Ask}(t)\}
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Best Ask}(t) = \min\{p_i | q_i > 0, p_i > \text{Best Bid}(t)\}
    \end{equation}
    \label{def:best_bid_ask_prices}
\end{definition}

The natural difference between the best bid and ask prices is called
the \textit{bid-ask spread}, which represents the immediate cost of
executing a trade aggressively\footnote{\cite[See][, p.70,
    ]{cartea2015algorithmic} \cite[See ][, p.35-36]{demsetz1968cost}} and
is a key indicator of market liquidity. The bid-ask spread is defined
as the difference between the best ask price and the best bid price,
and it reflects the cost that market participants incur when
executing a trade. A narrower bid-ask spread indicates a more liquid
market, where there is a smaller cost to trading, while a wider
bid-ask spread indicates a less liquid market, where there is a
larger cost to trading.

\begin{definition}[(Quoted) Bid-ask spread]
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Bid-ask spread}_{t} = P^{\text{bid}}_{t} - P^{\text{ask}}_{t}
    \end{equation}
    \label{def:bid_ask_spread}
\end{definition}
From the full bid-ask spread, the half spread can be derived, which
represents the cost of trading for a single side of the market, either
buying or selling.
\begin{definition}[(Quoted) Half spread]\label{def:half_spread}
    \begin{equation}
        {(H_{t})_{t \geqslant 0}} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \text{Bid-ask spread}_{t}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

This means that incoming MOs can potentially deplete the entire
volume at the best bid or ask price, leading to a subsequent
mid-price movement. The mid-price is defined as the arithmetic
average of the best bid and ask prices and often treated as a proxy
for the true underlying price of the asset. It assesses how close the
price of a trade is to the ´true underlying market
price´.\footnote{\cite[See][, p.71]{cartea2015algorithmic}, of course
    in some situations the true market price may lie closer to either the
    best bid or ask than the mid-price would suggest.}

\begin{definition}[Mid-price]\label{def:mid_price}
    \begin{equation}
        (S_{t})_{t \geqslant 0} = \frac{1}{2}\cdot(P^{\text{bid}}_{t} + P^{\text{ask}}_{t})
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

The best bid and best ask can also be expressed
as:\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.80]{luckner2021hawkes}}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:best_bid_half_spread}
    \text{Best Bid}(t) = S_{t} - H_{t}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:best_ask_half_spread}
    \text{Best Ask}(t) = S_{t} + H_{t}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Market Order (MO)]
    A \textit{buy} (respective \textit{sell}) market order is
    \label{def:market_order}
\end{definition}

In contrast, participants sending a LO specify a price at which they
are willing to buy or sell a certain quantity of electricity, and
these orders are added to the LOB and wait for a matching
counterparty to execute the trade. LOs are also referred to as
\textit{passive orders}, as they provide liquidity to the market by
adding volume to the LOB, rather than immediately executing against
existing orders.

\begin{definition}[Limit Order (LO)]
    A \textit{buy} (respective \textit{sell}) limit order is
    \label{def:limit_order}
\end{definition}

How the incoming MO are matched with resting LO to result in a trade,
is determined by the matching mechanism of the exchange system. Often
this algorithm is based on the price-time priority principle,
according to which orders are ranked based on their price and the
time they were submitted, just as the name already suggests
\cite{cartea2015algorithmic}. Then the MO is matched with the best
available LO in the LOB, which is the LO with the highest price for a
buy MO and the LO with the lowest price for a sell MO. If there are
multiple LO at the same price level, the MO is matched with the LO
that was submitted first, following the time priority principle. This
process continues until the MO is fully executed or there are no more
matching LO available in the LOB. When the volume of the MO exceeds
the available volume at the best price level, the remaining volume of
the MO is then matched with the next best price level, and this
process continues until the entire MO is executed or there are no
more matching LO available in the LOB. This process is also referred
to as \textit{walking the book}.

If a sell-MO is executed against a buy-LO, it is said to \textit{hit
    the bid}, while if a buy-MO is executed against a sell-LO, it is said
to \textit{lift the ask}.

Besides the discussed basic order types, there are also other
modalities available:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Iceberg Orders}: These are large orders that are split into smaller visible portions, allowing traders to hide the true size of their order from the market.
    \item \textbf{Stop Orders}: These are orders that become active only when a certain price level is reached, allowing traders to limit their losses or lock in profits.
    \item \textbf{Fill or Kill (FOK) Orders}: These are orders that must be executed immediately in their entirety or not at all, ensuring that traders either get the full quantity they want or no trade occurs.
    \item \textbf{Immediate or Cancel (IOC) Orders}: These are orders that must be executed immediately, but any portion of the order that cannot be filled is canceled, allowing traders to get as much of their order filled as possible without waiting for the entire order to be executed.
    \item \textbf{All or None (AON) Orders}: These are orders that must be executed in their entirety, but they do not have to be executed immediately, allowing traders to ensure that they get the full quantity they want without having to worry about partial fills.
    \item \textbf{Market Sweep Orders}: These are orders that are designed to quickly execute against multiple price levels in the LOB, allowing traders to quickly fill large orders without having to worry about the time it takes for the order to be executed.
\end{itemize}

Once an order has been submitted, the trader has the following three
actions available, given that the order is placed in the LOB:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cancel}: the order is removed from the LOB and it ceases to exist.
    \item \textbf{Modify}: the trader can modify the price or quantity of the order, and in the case of an iceberg order, the visible quantity. When the price or quantity is modified, the order loses priority in the queue.
    \item \textbf{Deactivate/Activate}: the trader can also choose to ’hibernate’ i.e. deactivate the order. In the case of hibernation the order is not visible in the LOB and can not be matched against. The reason why someone would like to hibernate their order could be, for example, in the case of losing a connection to the exchange.
\end{itemize}

Explaining the top six \emph{aggressive} order types more
closely\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.15]{oxford_hawkes_2020}}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The placement of a limit order inside the bid-ask spread. This will
          cause the price to decrease/in- crease if it is a sell/buy limit
          order placed below/above the best ask/bid price.
    \item The cancellation of the last remaining limit order in the first queue
          on either the bid/ask side of the book. The cancellation of such a
          sell/buy limit order will cause the price to increase/decrease.
    \item The execution of the last remaining limit order at either the best
          bid/ask price. This corresponds to the placement of a sell/buy market
          order at the best bid/ask price that consumes all of the available
          liquidity at that price and has the same effect on the price as the
          cancellation of the limit order.
\end{enumerate}
To describe ONLY the top of the book, the following system can be used (ONLY top book queues)
According to \cite{law2015pure} we can write: \(N(t) = (N_{1}(t),
N_{2}(t), \ldots, N_{12}(t))\) as the multivariate point process of
the twelve order types. It further follows that, we can infer the
absolute numbers of the shaping variables: \(M_{a}(t)\) as the buy
market orders, \(M_{b}(t)\) as the sell market orders, \(S_{a}(t)\)
as the ask price and \(S_{b}(t)\) as the bid price. As the tick size,
\(\delta\), is fixed and each (minimal) price jump is one tick, the
relations hold:
\begin{equation}
    M_{a}(t) = N_{1}(t) + N_{5}(t)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    M_{b}(t) = N_{2}(t) + N_{6}(t)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    S_{a}(t) = S_{a}(0) + (N_{1}(t) + N_{4}(t) - N_{12}(t)) \cdot \delta
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    S_{b}(t) = S_{b}(0) + (N_{11}(t) - N_{2}(t) - N_{3}(t)) \cdot \delta
\end{equation}

Therefore the top book spread evolves as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Spread}(t) = S_{a}(t) - S_{b}(t) = \text{Spread}(0) + (N_{1}(t) + N_{4}(t) - N_{12}(t)) \cdot \delta - (N_{11}(t) - N_{2}(t) - N_{3}(t)) \cdot \delta
\end{equation}

In order to avoid a situation where the spread would became less than
$\delta$, we impose that the intensities of the aggressive LO
additions are conditional on the spread based on the following
relationship, where $\mathbb{1}$ is the indicator function:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_{i}(t) = \lambda_{i}^{'}(t) \mathbb{1}(S(t) > \delta), \quad i = 3, 4
\end{equation}

So when the spread is equal to one tick, the intensity of aggressive
LO additions drops to zero. Now, it is clear that our model is
direction and timing consistent, but since we do not keep track of
the depths of the price levels, the model is not volume consistent.

See here: \cite[][, p.36]{korhonen2025market}: In order for a LOB
model to be consistent with the real-world LOB dynamics it must
satisfy the following three rules:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Direction consistency}:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item On the arrival of a marketable buy (sell) order, only the ask (bid)
                    price can move up (down), while the bid (ask) price has to stay
                    unchanged:
              \item On the arrival of limit buy (sell) order, only the bid (ask) price
                    can move up (down) while the ask (bid) price has to stay unchanged,
                    given that the LO arrives inside the spread. Otherwise, both the bid
                    and ask prices will remain unchanged:
              \item On the cancellation of a limit buy (sell) order, only the bid (ask)
                    price can move down (up) if the canceled order is the only order at
                    the best bid (ask), while the ask (bid) price will remain unchanged.
                    Otherwise, both the bid and ask prices will remain unchanged:
          \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Timing consistency}: the bid and ask prices can only move at times when orders
          arrive or are cancelled:
    \item \textbf{Volume consistency}:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item On the arrival of marketable buy (sell) order whose volume is larger
                    than the available liquidity at current best ask (bid), the price
                    moves up (down) and otherwise stays unchanged:
              \item If the volume of the canceled limit buy (sell) order is equal to the
                    volume of volume available at the current best bid (ask), the bid
                    (ask) price moves down (up), and otherwise the price stays unchanged:
          \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
One could argue that these rules only apply when no orders can arrive at the same exact moment, but given how the matching engine of the exchange operates the orders are always handled sequentially even in the case that they arrive on the same exact nanosecond. The conditions stated above are necessary in order to model the LOB realistically, regardless of any assumptions about the arrival rates or volume distributions. However, in practice achieving a consistent LOB model is extremely difficult since we would need to keep track of the depths of all the price levels on both sides. In the next section we present the weakly consistent LOB model which only models the best bid and ask queues, and is thus only direction and timing consistent.

\section{The Market Making Problem}\label{sec:market_making_problem}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Market Making essentially consists of posting limit orders
continuously on both sides of the LOB providing liquidity to the
market, at rapid speeds, and earning the Bid-Ask spread as
compensation.\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.1; ]{jain2025impulse}
    \cite[See ][, p.9 and 20 and also the important specification of MM
        and Liquidity Privision.]{cartea2015algorithmic}}

The rational behind this way of providing
immediacy\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.]{demsetz1968cost}} can be
motivated by considering an information based perespective on market
participants. A broad classification can be made between
\textit{informed} and \textit{uninformed} traders, where the former
have access to private information that allows them to make
(suspectively) more informed trading decisions.\footnote{\cite[See
    ][, p.30]{cartea2015algorithmic}} Market Makers (MM) are typically
considered to be \textit{uninformed} traders, as they do not have
access to private information and instead rely on the public
information available in the market. However, they are still exposed
to the risk of trading with \textit{informed} traders, who may have
an informational advantage and can exploit the MM's quotes to their
benefit. This creates a situation of \textit{information asymmetry},
where the MM is at a disadvantage compared to informed traders. These
information asymmetries directly translate into the following sources
of involved risks a MM faces:
% Maybe also rather in a table than in a bullet list
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adverse Selection Risk}: This risk arises when the MM trades with informed traders who have access to private information that allows them to make more informed trading decisions. The MM may end up buying at a higher price or selling at a lower price than the true value of the asset, resulting in potential losses.
    \item \textbf{Inventory Risk}: This risk arises from holding an inventory of assets that may fluctuate in value due to market conditions. The MM may be forced to sell at a loss if the market moves against their position, resulting in potential losses.
    \item \textbf{Latency Risk}: This risk arises from delays in receiving and processing market data, which can result in the MM's quotes being stale and not reflecting the current market conditions. This can lead to potential losses if the MM is unable to react quickly enough to changing market conditions.
\end{itemize}

This perspective is also justified by looking at the different
participant types at the EPEX SPOT, where there are not only utilitiy
companies but also financial institutions, which are typically
considered to be more informed traders, as they have access to more
sophisticated trading strategies.

Now balancing these types of risks is the core problem of market
making, as the MM must continuously adjust their quotes to manage
their inventory and minimize their exposure to adverse selection and
latency risks, while still providing liquidity to the market. The
main appoaches on how to come up with optimal strategies for market

\subsection{Volumes and Volatilty}\label{sub_sec:volumes_and_volatility}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The volume pattern over a trading session or lifetime of a contract
for the intraday power markets is quite distinctive from other
financial markets, that typically show a U-shape over the course of a
normal trading day. Starting off with high volumes near market
opening, a slight volume lull in the middle of the day, and a peak in
volume near market close.\footnote{By volume we refer to the trading
    volume measured in MWh.} This shape generally reflects the quote
updates based on newly arrived information. In contrast, the intraday
power markets show a different pattern, with a peak in volume near
gate closure, but also a significant increase in volume during the
last hour of trading.\footnote{With activity, also the volume keeps
    up, as 80\% of total volume is traded within the last three hours of
    a trading period \cite[See ][, p.238]{balardy2022empirical}.}This is
due to the fact that market participants have more information about
the supply and demand conditions for the delivery period as it
approaches, and they adjust their positions accordingly
(\textit{trading against information arrival}). It can also be seen
clearly that the volume picks up at around 6 to 7 hours prior to gate
closure.\footnote{This is usually the time when the morning shift
    starts for traders in Europe, as the market is open 24/7, the trading
    is done in three shifts: morning, evening and night shifts or
    alternatively, the night shift might be working from a country with a
    more suitable time-zone like Singapore}. Also algorithmns may try to
close out any remaining residual volumes at the end of the trading
sessions, what possibly leads to an increase in volumes towards gate
closure in combination with execution strategies that try to postpone
non-urgent transactions to times when spreads are narrow and more
preferable, what is also the case near gate
closure.\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.66]{cartea2015algorithmic}} This
would match the observed tightening of the spread that can be seen in
\hyperref[sub_sec:spreads]{3.2.3 Spreads}.

a lower mean bid-ask spread of about 2.97€/MWh. As the durations of
the trading sessions per product differ in their lengths, a smooting
of the bid-ask spread can be observed, the longer the trading session
lasts \cite[See ][, p.238]{balardy2022empirical}. Concluding, the
bid-ask spread generally follows more like an L-shape instead of a
U-shape pattern as is usually observed in other financial markets

of the bid-ask spread, increased volatility positively affects the
bid-ask spread, especially pronounced for off-peak produtcs
\cite[See][, p.246]{balardy2022empirical}. Not surprisingly,
fundamental variables like load, wind, solar and their associated
forecast errors (positive and negative) have a negative impact for
the bid-ask spread \cite[See][, p.247]{balardy2022empirical}. The
Bid-ask spread has two important dependency relationships: First, the
impact of the Bid-ask spread on posted LO depths can't be ignored,
since the bid-ask spread is rarely tick size and often exceeds it by
several magnitudes.\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.78]{luckner2021hawkes}}
Second, it is relevant for the arrivals of MO, as we have seen that
they appear in situations where the spread is favorable for them and
narrow.

\chapter{Mathematical Modelling}\label{ch:mathematical_modelling}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Centers around Order Flow Modelling
In order to realistically model the LOB dynamics and derive the
optimal market making strategy, we need to choose a suitable
mathematical framework that can replicate the key empirical
characteristics of the LOB. A natural framework is offered by the
Hawkes-Process family, which is a class of self-exciting point
processes that can capture the clustering and dependence structure of
order arrivals in the LOB.\footnote{Note: In theory the order arrival
intensities can be any predictable non-negative stochastic processes, \cite[see ][, p.39]{korhonen2025market}.} By also including cross-excitation among
different order types, this process family also offers modelling
capabilities for complex interactions.

Explicitly not assuming that the MM can adjust bid and ask prices
continuously, the MM's decision problem is formulated as a form of an
optimal impulse-control switching problem, see \cite{law2015pure}.

Not only is this assumption not realistic but also not properly
penalised in the existing discussions, as altering order parameters
means losing the queue position due to the applied price-time
priority principle most exchanges operate on.

In the following sections, we will first introduce the basic concepts
of Hawkes processes, as member of the general point process family,
and then describe how we can use this framework to model the order
arrivals and mid-price dynamics in the electricity markets,
ultimately leading to the derivation of the optimal market making
strategy. A complete taxonomy of the main process families belongig
to the point processes is given below.

\section{The Point Process Family}\label{sec:point_process_family}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Starting with the basic concepts of the general point process family,
we can define a point process as a random collection of points in
time, where each point represents the occurrence of an event. This
basic model for representing event streams assumes that their
occurrence is random, i.e. independent and can be described by a
probability
distribution.\footnote{\cite{palm1943intensitatsschwankungen}}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Homogeneous Point Processes}\label{sub_sec:homogeneous_point_processes}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\cite[See ][, p.3]{oxford_hawkes_2020}
\begin{definition}[Point process]\label{def:point_process}
    A point process is a stochastic process $(T_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$
    taking values in $\mathbb{R}_{+}$ such that for every $k\in\mathbb{N}$, $T_k \leq T_{k+1}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Counting Process]\label{def:counting_process}
    Given a point process $(T_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$, its counting process is defined by
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:counting_function}
        N(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{1}_{\{T_k\le t\}}
    \end{equation}
    and counts the number of events that have occurred up to time $t$. The
    process is called \emph{simple} if no two events occur at the same time,
    i.e., $\mathbb{P}(T_k=T_j)=0$ for all $k\neq j$.
\end{definition}
In reality of course, multiple incoming messages can have the exact same assigned timestamp,
suggesting a \emph{non-simple} point process. However, for the sake of simplicity,
we will assume a simple point process in our modelling framework, as these cases are very
rare and only make up x \% of observations (timestamped on nanoseconds).\footnote{Assume no two types
    of orders can arrive at the exact same time, if the exchange operates multiple
    servers it can of course accept two incoming order types at the same instant, \cite[see ][p. 45]{law2015pure}}

An important characteristic of the counting process is its so-called
\emph{conditional intensity}:

\begin{definition}[Conditional Intensity]\label{def:conditional_intensity}
    The conditional intensity $\lambda(t)$ of a counting process $N(t)$ is defined as the limit
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:conditional_intensity}
        \lambda(t) := \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\mathbb{P}(N(t+\Delta t)-N(t)=1|\mathcal{F}_t)}{\Delta t}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathcal{F}_t$ is the filtration generated by the counting process up to time $t$,
    i.e. $\mathcal{F}_t : = \sigma(N(s): 0 \leq s \leq t)$.
\end{definition}

Another important quantity in the study of point processes is the
point-wise integral of the conditional intensity of the process,
$\Lambda(t)$, i.e.
\begin{definition}[Compensator]\label{def:compensator}
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:compensator}
        \Lambda(t) := \int_0^t \lambda(s) ds
    \end{equation}
    We call this value the compensator of the process, and we have the
    following useful result about compensators (Lemma X.Y.Z in XX).
\end{definition}
% This is the regular compensator for martingales (Doob Decomposition)
\begin{theorem}[$\mathcal{F}$-Martingale]\label{thm:f_martingale}
    Suppose the $\mathcal{F}_t$-adapted counting process $(N(t))_{t \in \left[0, \infty \right)}$ has a left-continuous
    intensity $\lambda(t)$. Then the process $M(t) := N(t) - \Lambda(t)$
    is an $\mathcal{F}_t$-martingale, i.e. for every $0 \leq s \leq t$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:f_martingale}
        \mathbb{E}[M(t) | \mathcal{F}_s] = M(s).
    \end{equation}
    Counting processes preserve three notions of regularities, namely:
    \emph{stationarity}, \emph{simplicity} and \emph{orderliness}.\\
    A counting process $N$ is said to be (strictly) \emph{stationary} if $N(t + s) - N(s)$
    has the same distribution for all $s \geq 0$. $N$ is said to be \emph{simple} when:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbb{P}(N({t}) = \textit{0 or 1 for all t}) = 1
    \end{equation}
    and orderly, wehn it is first stationary and:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbb{P}(N(t+h) - N(t) \geq 2) = o(h) \textit{as h} \rightarrow 0
    \end{equation}.
\end{theorem}
One of the most prominent members of counting processes is the \emph{Possion Process},
that can be defined in serveral ways.
Based on its intensity it may be defined as:
\begin{definition}[Possion Process]\label{def:possion_process}
    Let $N$ be a counting process and consider a sequence $\left(s_{i},{} t_{i}\right]$
    of half-open intervals with $0 \leq s_{i} < t_{i} \leq s_{i+1}$, $i \in \{1, \ldots, k\}$.
    $N$ is called a Poisson process with intensity $\lambda > 0$ if:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbb{P}[N(t_{i}) - N(s_{i}) = n_{i}, i \in {1, \ldots, k}] = \prod_{i=1}^{k} \frac{(\lambda(t_{i}-s_{i}))^{n_{i}}}{n_{i}!} e^{-\lambda(t_{i}-s_{i})}
    \end{equation}

    It follows from this definition that a Possion Process satisfies the
    following conditions:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The number of events in an interval $\left[s, t\right),~0 \leq s <
                  t$, follows a Poisson distribution with mean $\lambda (t-s)$
        \item the process has \emph{independent} increments, i.e. the number of
              arrivals in disjoint intervals are independent.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
Allowing the intensity $\lambda$ to vary with time, we transition from
the homogeneous to the non-homogeneous Possion Process.\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.4]{oxford_hawkes_2020}}

\subsection{Non Homogeneous Point Processes}\label{sub_sec:non_homogeneous_point_processes}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
In a non-homogenous Poisson process, the (infinitesimal) probability
of an event happening at time t may vary with t, but it is still
independent of other events.\footnote{\cite[See ][,
        p.1]{mei2017neural}}

An importnat characteristic of the Possion Process is its
\emph{memorylessness}, i.e. the distribution of future arrival times
only depends on information available at the current time $t$, and
not what happened in the past leading to $t$.\footnote{\cite[See ][,
        p.4]{oxford_hawkes_2020}} To adjust for this \emph{memoryless}
property, a historic dependency structure has to be introduced,
leading to the \emph{Hawkes-Process}.
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{One Dimensional Hawkes Processes}\label{sub_sec:one_dimensional_hawkes_processes}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A Hawkes process\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.]{hawkes1971spectra}}
(Liniger, 2009) supposes that past events can temporarily raise the
probability of future events, assuming that such excitation is
positive, additive over the past events, and exponentially decaying
with time, see \hyperref{thm:f_martingale}{Theorem
    1}.\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.1]{mei2017neural}} Real world scenarios
often violate these assumptions, as an event can also be inhibitious
rather than excitatory, and the excitation effect may not be strictly
additive over time as effect strengths might dampen down or that an
exponentially decay can't be guaranteed.\footnote{\cite[See][,
        p.2]{mei2017neural}} The output of a Hawkes process is essentially an
event intensity, which is an instantantaneous
probability.\footnote{\cite[See][, p.2]{mei2017neural}}

\begin{definition}[Hawkes Process]\label{def:hawkes_process}
    A Hawkes process is a simple point process with a conditional intensity function $\lambda(t)$
    defined as:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:hawkes_intensity}
        \lambda(t) = \mu + \int_{t_i < t} \phi(t - s) dN_{s}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^+$ is the baseline intensity of the process,
    $\phi(t): \mathbb{R}^+ \to \mathbb{R}^+$ is the excitation kernel function, and $t_i$ are the times of past events.
\end{definition}\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.5]{oxford_hawkes_2020}}

In dimension $d = 1$, the process exhibits \emph{self-excitation},
where past event occurences, spark an increase in the likelihood of
observing events in the near future. Note that in $d=1$, only one
specific type of event is considered for the self-excitation. It is
of course possible and rather plausible, that in realistsic
scenarios, multiple types of events are responsible for future
excitations, proposing the need to incporporate a dependency
structure for mutual event type impacts.\footnote{\cite[See ][,
        p.5]{oxford_hawkes_2020}}
% This is the exact reason why we need a d-dimensional framework, since for a specific considered event
% type, like MO arrivals, excitation of additional future MO arrivals also depends on the other queues like LO arrivals, Cancellations
To motivate a later extension, in effect, Hawkes processes and
continuous-time Markov chains can have either an event viewpoint or a
state viewpoint, respectively.\footnote{See \cite{morariu2018hybrid}
    p.2}

\subsection{Multi Dimensional Hawkes Processes}\label{sub_sec:multi_dimensional_hawkes_processes}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\cite[See ][,p.5]{oxford_hawkes_2020}:

\begin{definition}[Multivariate Hawkes Process]\label{def:multivariate_hawkes_process}
    A $d$-dimensional counting process $N = (N_1, \ldots, N_d)$, is a
    multivariate Hawkes process when:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:multivariate_hawkes_intensity}
        \lambda_{i}(t) = \mu_{i} + \sum_{j = 1}^{d} \int_{0}^{t} \phi_{ij}(t - s) dN_{j, s}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mu_{i} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are the baseline intensities, $t_i$ are the times of past events and
    $\phi_{ij}(t): \mathbb{R}^+ \to \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ are the excitation kernel functions,
    and satisfy:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\phi_{ij}(t) \geq 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$ and $i, j \in \{1, \ldots, d\}$
        \item $\phi_{ij}(t) = 0$ for all $t \leq 0$ and $i, j \in \{1, \ldots, d\}$
        \item $\phi_{ij}(t)$ is locally integrable for all $i, j \in \{1, \ldots, d\}$ i.e. $\phi_{ij}(t) \in L_{1, loc}(\Omega)$
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

As in XX, two alternative notations of
\hyperref[eq:multivariate_hawkes_intensity]{Equation 4.9} can be
considered, allowing for a more compact notation and extended
interpretation of its components. The first one, being the
\emph{convolutional} notation:
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda(t) = \mu + \Phi * dN_t
\end{equation}
where $\Phi * dN_t$ is the convolution of the kernel function $\Phi$ with the counting process $dN_t$.
The second one, being the \emph{matrix} notation:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda(t) = \mu + \int_{0}^{t} \Phi(t - s) dN_{s}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Adding State Dependencies and Marks}\label{sub_sec:adding_state_dependencies_and_marks}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
After having discussed the one- and d-dimensional Hawkes Process, the
state dependent and marked variants should be introduced.
Understanding how these two properties are essential for a realistic
modelling of the LOB dynamics, we must first differentiate them along
two dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item where information originates from and
    \item how it affects the intensity ($\lambda_{t}$) of future order
          arrivals.
\end{enumerate}

Adding state dependency and marks is also motivated by the findings
here: \cite[see ][, p.83]{cartea2015algorithmic} where we clearyl see
that the size of an order (so its magnitude) impacts the arrival
rates of after following orders heavily. Here it is alos suggested
that order arrivals seem to be followed by further order arrivals on
both sides of the book, and more on its own side than on the opposing
side \cite[see ][, p.83]{cartea2015algorithmic}.

\subsubsection{The Marked Hawkes Process (MHP)}\label{sub_sub_sec:the_marked_hawkes_process}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The Marked Hawkes Process can be thought of as containing
Event-Driven Info. The intensity changes based on a specific property
attached directly to an incoming event at the exact millisecond it
arrives. An incoming order has attributes like its size, direction,
and price level, which are all relevant for the future order arrival
intensity. The Marked Hawkes Process is therefore a natural extension
of the standard Hawkes Process, where the intensity is only a
function of the past event times.

\begin{paragraph}{Mathematical formulation}
    Every time an event \(i\) occurs at time
    \(t_{i}\), it carries a "mark" (\(m_{i}\)). In market microstructure,
    this mark is almost always the Order Size / Volume or Trade
    Direction:
    \begin{equation}
        \lambda_{t} = \mu + \sum_{t_{i}<t} \phi(t-t_{i}, m_{i})
    \end{equation}
\end{paragraph}

The intuition behind including marks is that the impact of an event
on future order arrivals is not only determined by its occurrence but
also by its characteristics. For example, a large market order
(carrying the mark $m_{i}$) may have a more significant impact on the
afterfollowing excitation ripple (a bigger shock through the kernel
function: \(\phi(t-t_{i}, m_{i})\)) and thus a higher intensity of
future orders than a small MO, especially considering situations
where the Market-depth is thin. In this thesis, we will explore if
incorporating the \emph{Volume} of an order as mark will carry any
beneficial information for the future order arrival intensity.

Memorywise: The system only looks backward at the history of
individual events and their specific sizes.
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{The State Dependent Hawkes Process (SDHP)}\label{sub_sub_sec:the_state_dependent_hawkes_process}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The State-Dependent Hawkes Process represents Environment-Driven
Info. The intensity changes based on the ambient state of the entire
system at time \(t\), regardless of which specific event caused that
state.

\begin{paragraph}{Mathematical formulation}
    Introduced to fix a major flaw in pure
    Hawkes models: traditional Hawkes processes ignore the geometry of
    the order book. In a state-dependent model, the kernel itself is
    modulated by a piecewise constant state variable, \(X_{t}\),
    representing the continuous state of the LOB.

    \begin{equation}
        \lambda_{t} = \mu(X_{t}) + \sum_{t_{i}<t} \phi_{X_{t_{i}}}(t-t_{i}, X_{t})
    \end{equation}
\end{paragraph}

The intuition behind including state dependencies is that the state
\(X_{t}\) could be the bid-ask spread or the queue imbalance. If the
spread is at its minimum tick size, the probability of a
price-improving limit order dropping in is physically zero. The model
dampens or accelerates intensities based on the geometry of the book
layout when the order hits.

Memorywise: The system is fully coupled. The arrival of an order
changes the book state (\(X_{t}\)), and the new book state instantly
updates the baseline (\(\mu \)) and excitation physics (\(\Phi \)) of
future orders.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{The Unified Approach: The State dependent marked Hawkes Process (SDMHP)}\label{sub_sub_sec:the_unified_approach}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
In a combined model, the intensity \(\lambda _{t}\) of order arrivals
depends simultaneously on the changing geometry of the book
(\(X_{t}\)) and the specific properties of past historical trades
(\(m_{i}\)).

\begin{paragraph}{Mathematical formulation}
    \begin{equation}
        \lambda_{t} = \mu(X_{t}) + \sum_{t_{i}<t} \phi_{X_{t_{i}}}(t-t_{i}, m_{i}, X_{t})
    \end{equation}
    with the following components:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \(X_{t}\): The state of the LOB at time \(t\), which can include variables such as the bid-ask spread, queue imbalance, and other relevant market conditions.
        \item \(m_{i}\): The mark associated with the \(i\)-th event, which can represent characteristics such as order size or trade direction.
        \item \(\mu(X_{t})\): The baseline intensity, which is a function of the current state of the LOB, capturing the underlying rate of order arrivals based on the current market conditions.
        \item \(\phi_{X_{t_{i}}}(t-t_{i}, m_{i}, X_{t})\): The coupled excitation kernel, which depends on the time since the last event \(t-t_{i}\), the mark \(m_{i}\), and the state of the LOB at the time of the event \(X_{t}\).
    \end{itemize}
\end{paragraph}

This combined approach allows to properly model such cases:
\begin{quote}
    A huge trade (large mark \(m_{i}\)) just executed. Because
    the bid-ask spread is currently wide (state \(X_{t}\)), this massive
    trade will trigger a cascade of aggressive cancel-and-replace orders
    (\(\phi_{X_{t_{i}}}\) becomes heavily excited). However, if the
    spread were narrow, that exact same size trade would trigger virtually
    no reaction.
\end{quote}

\section{Extension: The Neural Hawkes Process (NHP)}\label{sec:extension_neural_hawkes_process}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
While computationally beautiful, implementing a State-Dependent
Marked Hawkes process presents a massive hurdle: Parameter Explosion.
If you track a limit order book with 10 distinct states (e.g.,
deciles of queue imbalance) and use 3 types of marks (small, medium,
and large orders), your kernel combinations multiply exponentially.
Estimating these parameters in real time using Maximum Likelihood
Estimation (MLE) or optimization methods like the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker
(KKT) conditions becomes computationally intensive for high-frequency
setups. To solve this, modern quantitative research frequently
utilizes Neural Hawkes Processes (NHP). Instead of computing explicit
mathematical formulas for every state-mark combination, deep learning
architectures (like Continuous-Time LSTMs) digest the raw sequence of
states and marks together, outputting a dynamically changing arrival
intensity natively.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:conditional_intensities}
    \lambda_{i}(t) = \mu_{i}(t, X_{t}) + \sum_{j=1}^{6} \int_{0}^{t} \phi_{ij}(t - s) dN_{j}(s)
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\lambda_{i}(t)$ conditional arrival intensity of event type $i$,
    \item $\mu_{i}(t, X_{t})$ is the baseline intensity for process $i$, which can depend on time $t$ and the current state of the limit order book $X_{t}$,
    \item $\phi_{ij}(t - s)$ is the excitation kernel function that captures the influence of from event type $j$ to event type $i$,
    \item $X_{t}$ state vector, e.g. spread, midprice, imbalance, time-to-close at time $t$.
\end{itemize}
The mid-price and spread dynamics can be formulated as follows.
Define the mid-price as $P_{t}$ and the Spread as $S_{t}$ as pure jump processes, driven
by the price-moving events:
\begin{equation}
    dP_{t} = \sum_{i=1}^{6} \Delta_{i}^{P} dN_{i}(t)
\end{equation}
and the spread as:
\begin{equation}
    dS_{t} = \sum_{i=1}^{6} \Delta_{i}^{S} dN_{i}(t)
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\Delta_{i}^{P}$ is the price impact of event type $i$ on the mid-price,
    \item $\Delta_{i}^{S}$ impact of event $i$ on the spread.
\end{itemize}

State dependency can be added by describing the state using the
possible variables combined as:
\begin{equation}
    X_{t} = (S_{t}, P_{t}, I_{t}, Q_{t}^{L1}, Q_{t}^{L2}, Q_{t}^{L3}, T_{t}, V_{t})
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $S_{t}$ is the bid-ask spread at time $t$
    \item $P_{t}$ is the mid-price at time $t$
    \item $I_{t}$ is the order imbalance at time $t$
    \item $Q_{t}^{L1}, Q_{t}^{L2}, Q_{t}^{L3}$ are the queue sizes at levels 1, 2, and 3 of the limit order book at time $t$
    \item $T_{t}$ is the time-to-delivery at time $t$
    \item $V_{t}$ is the volatility regime at time $t$
\end{itemize}

Then a baseline can be chosen as:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{i}(t, X_{t}) = f_{i}(S_{t}, P_{t}, I_{t}, Q_{t}^{L1}, Q_{t}^{L2}, Q_{t}^{L3}, T_{t}, V_{t})
\end{equation}
or alternatively:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{i}(t, X_{t}) = exp(\alpha_{i} + \beta_{i}^{\top}X_{t}+g_{i}(t))
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\alpha_{i}$ channel-specific level,
    \item $\beta_{i}$ sensitivity to the state variables,
    \item $g_{i}(t)$ deterministic intraday seasonality..
\end{itemize}

Including marks $m_{k}$ to each event, for example the order size as
mark, a marked kernel can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    \phi_{ij}(u, m) = a_{i, j} h(m) e^{-\omega_{i, j} u} \mathbb{1}_{u > 0}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $a_{i, j}$ strength of excitation,
    \item $\omega_{i, j}$ decay speed,
    \item $h(m)$ mark impact function, e.g. $h(m) = m$ or $h(m) = \log(1+m)$
\end{itemize}
what lets larger MOs or LOs have bigger effects than smaller ones.

The level resolved extension results from refining each aggregated
event type further by side, event type and level, resulting in a
18-dimensional Hawkes process: $i \in$ \{side x type x level\} with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item side $\in$ \{bid, ask\},
    \item type $\in$ \{MO, LO, CA\},
    \item level $\in$ \{1, 2, 3\}.
\end{itemize}
with the intensities becoming:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_{i}(t) = \mu_{i}(t, X_{t}) + \sum_{j=1}^{18} \int_{0}^{t} \phi_{ij}(t - s, m) dN_{j}(s),\\
    i = 1, \ldots, 18
\end{equation}

Stability conditions: For exponential kernels, define the integrated
excitation matrix $G$ as:
\begin{equation}
    G_{ij} = \int_{0}^{\infty} \phi_{ij}(u) du = \frac{a_{i, j}}{\omega_{i, j}}
\end{equation}
A sufficient stability condition is $\rho(G) < 1$
with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\rho(G)$ is the spectral radius of the matrix $G$,
    \item $G_{ij}$ is the expected number of events of type $i$ triggered by a single event of type $j$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stochastic Optimal Control}\label{sub_sec:stochastic_optimal_control}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The goal of stochastic control problems is to maximise (minimise)
some expected profit (cost) function by tuning a strategy which
itself affects the dynamics of the underlying stochastic system and
to find the strategy which attains the maximum
(minimum).\footnote{\cite[See ][, p.100]{cartea2015algorithmic}}

A prominent tool for solving such problems is the \emph{dynamic
    programming principle} (DPP) and the related non-linear partial
differential equation (PDE), also knwon as
\emph{Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman} (HJB) equation.\footnote{\cite[See ][,
        p.100]{cartea2015algorithmic}} With the DPP a stochastic control
problem can be solved by breaking it down into a sequence of smaller
subproblems, which can be solved recursively. The HJB equation
provides a necessary condition for optimality and can be used to
derive the optimal control strategy by solving the PDE subject to
appropriate boundary conditions.\footnote{\cite[See ][,
        p.100]{cartea2015algorithmic}}

In the context of this thesis, we are especially interested in the
setting where a MM wishes to maximise expected terminal wealth by
using MO and LO.

\section{Exchange Latency}\label{sec:exchange_latency}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Here the exact calculation of the exchange latency is provided, which
is the time it takes for an order to be processed and executed on the
exchange after it has been submitted. This includes the time taken
for the order to reach the exchange, be processed by the exchange's
matching engine, and receive a confirmation of execution. The
exchange latency can vary based on several factors, including the
type of order, the current market conditions, and the specific
exchange being used. Understanding and minimizing exchange latency is
crucial for developing effective trading strategies, particularly in
high-frequency trading environments where even small delays can
impact profitability. important for sampling and fitting durations
etc.
